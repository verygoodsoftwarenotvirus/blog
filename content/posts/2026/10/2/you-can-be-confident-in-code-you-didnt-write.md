title: "You can be confident in code you didn't write"
date: "2026-10-02T12:48:49-05:00"
author: "verygoodsoftwarenotvirus"
---

I have this one repo I’ve been working on for literally the better part of a decade. It started off as an app idea, and my constant need to iterate and plug away at some kind of coding problem saw its feature set expand massively, while the customer count was a stagnant and perpetual zero. What started as a recipe management app that we’ve all built before became something that supported user analytics, email and password reset support, two factor authentication, webhooks, mobile device push notifications, etc.

Something I set up early on in that project’s history was a suite of integration tests that spin up containers for the Postgres and Redis instances it needs to connect to, runs an instance of the service pointed at them, and uses its own service’s client to make supported requests and validate outcomes. So it creates a user, then uses that user’s credentials to create a recipe, then asserts that it can read that recipe, asserts that it can update that recipe, and asserts that it can delete those recipes. Where there are relevant validation or access schemes, we add tests for each of those. I’m a firm believer that the function of testing is to codify expected behavior, and running this suite on every change allows me to be confident that the service meets its obligations and doesn’t experience regressions.

Accordingly, even before AI-assisted programming came into being, I would make changes involving moving a bunch of files around, or doing multiple regex query renames across the entire repository, and while the line changes were really large, I always felt confident that I hadn’t broken anything, because I had these tests that made sure of it.

Like many of us, I have been bitten by the AI “coding” bug, and have been plugging away at my TODO lists and project features with it. I found myself very earnestly reviewing every line changed by the bot, and when using prior models, I felt obligated to review every change in depth so I could verify nothing untoward was happening. This got very tiresome very quickly. I’m just as incapable of reviewing the massive quantity of output of these models produce as I am at matching it.

So I don’t anymore. I’ve gotten to a place where I basically just make sure there aren’t huge sweeping deletions, and I check in and read the code manually from time to time, both to bolster my understanding and assure that nothing stupid is afoot. This practice might not last much longer either, frankly. My tests are still there, they still run on every change. Their deletion or disabling would be the only thing that gives me some form of concern.

It reminds me of when I first learned about `go fmt`. A lot of people (myself included) had gleaned the lesson from `go fmt` that specific formatting configurations didn’t matter, only that the code was consistently formatted. My opinion is now basically that I don’t give a shit what any of it looks like. I don’t even particularly care if it’s formatted, so long as the AI can keep iterating on it.

I sort of think the folks who look down on me for this practice are desperate to be stuck in a time gone by. I view it as akin to an old-world cobbler walking into an Adidas factory and bristling at the lack of awls. Why would or should the factory care when they’re pumping out shoes at a pace and price no cobbler could match?

I’m *not* saying I’m the Adidas of programmers, and I still think knowing about programming is a valuable skill I will use to make money for the rest of my life. I just also use this tool to do it at a pace that is precluded by everything from mental illness, disorganization, and just being a normal human being. Claude writes largely much better code than I ever had the capacity to write, more thorough unit tests, catches edge cases and foolish misconceptions I would have gleefully committed and written tests around.

My role in the process has become more of that of a technical product manager. I know what the agents should be focusing on at a high level, I organize their work, I have them review and merge each others’ code, and then I use the software to make sure it does indeed match my expectations. It basically always has and does.

At work, I’ve been letting Claude do it’s thing as well, and our deployment processes keep us safe. We heavily feature flag things (a pattern Claude copies), we roll things out to dev and QA before merging them into production, and we have integration tests that run on real infrastructure to verify we’re not shipping bugs. I cannot think of a single feature I had Claude implement that actually went out and caused an outage. I can think of a couple of features that were completely hand-written that had to have feature flags flipped to save the day. That’s not me casting aspersions, rather asserting why I feel confident.

Have I deprived myself of some learning opportunity? I don’t really think so. I’m near my 40s, have two small children, and some attempt at a social life and fitness routine. The era of my life where I could stay up until 2AM reading RFCs to try and implement my own libraries and “better learn” protocols is over. If I had to stop and take the time to learn a completely new skill like mobile development, it would necessarily consume years of my life before I’d ever feel confident enough to try my hand at building something meaningful or joining a team that does so, and I simply do not have the time or patience for that anymore.

Claude, on the other hand, is very adept at writing iOS apps that get the job I want done. I don’t think if I spent a year looking at Swift tutorials and reading the documentation, that you would pick my hand-written code over what Claude can generate in like 5 minutes. That’s just the practical reality we’re all faced with now: hand-written code’s only real advantage is that sometimes, it is structured and organized in a way that very much behooves a human reader, and even that is incredibly contingent upon the writer.

I think ultimately what I realized is that what I liked out of my career was not writing the code, ti was shipping it. I had a job at a fledgling startup years ago that predated the AI coding era. That job gave me a ton of occupational trauma around deadlines, work/life balance, but we shipped a new product every 6 months, and it was invigorating to do so. I find myself thinking back on that time, not with a mournful regret at how caught up in all of it I became, but wondering if maybe the company wouldn’t have failed if we had been able to merge features at the pace possible today.
